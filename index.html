An open safety design for cricket, free for anyone to build

Flying bail injuries, never again.

A bail guard is a simple device that tethers cricket bails to the wicket, allowing them to lift and fall normally while preventing them from becoming dangerous projectiles. The design is freely published and available for anyone to build, use, manufacture or improve.

    01The problemWhy this matters

    Flying bails can cause severe injuries

    Bails are small, hard, and leave the stumps at speed, at head height for the people standing closest. These are not hypothetical cases.

    Mark BoucherSouth Africa's wicketkeeper. In 2012 a bail flew into his left eye. He lost the lens, iris and pupil, and had to retire immediately.
    Paul DowntonEngland wicketkeeper. A bail struck his eye in 1990 and partly shattered the lens. The resulting loss of depth perception ended his career.
    MS DhoniWhile batting, struck near the eye by a bail against Zimbabwe in 2016, described at the time as a lucky escape.
    David WarnerHit by a flying bail while fielding close in, during a Test at Galle.
    Mark HowardBroadcaster, hospitalized after being struck in a freak bail incident.

    7 personas are in range

    Up to half the field is in the firing line. Every single ball.

    WicketkeeperClosest of all. Bowled, stumped, run out, hit wicket
    BatterRun out, stumped, bowled, hit wicket
    Close in fieldersSilly point, short leg, slips, backing up for throws, hit wicket
    BowlerBacking up for throws
    InfieldersReceiving at the stumps
    Non strikerStraight drives, run outs
    Straight umpireStraight drives, run outs

    And behind every one of them, a parent, a teammate or a friend watching from the boundary.

    Children are the most exposed

    A younger player's eyeline is lower and closer to the bails, reactions are still developing, and softer tissue and bone means an impact does more damage. Most youth cricket is played without any eye protection at all.

    02The LawsAlready permitted

    The Laws already allow this

    This is not a proposal to change cricket. In 2017 the MCC wrote a provision into the Laws specifically for devices like this one.

    MCC Law 8.3.4, effective 1 October 2017
    “Devices aimed at protecting player safety by limiting the distance that a bail can travel off the stumps will be allowed, subject to the approval of the Governing Body for the match and the Ground Authority.”
    Laws of Cricket, 2017 Code, 3rd Edition 2022. Marylebone Cricket Club. ESPNcricinfo report

    A bail guard is exactly the device that clause describes. Using one needs no law change and no special dispensation, only the agreement of your league and your ground, which the Law already anticipates.

    03Open and freePublished, not patented

    Published, not patented

    We had something patentable and chose to publish it instead. The full design is archived on Zenodo as a defensive publication, first released in October 2025, under a Creative Commons Attribution licence. That makes it prior art, so the mechanism stays permanently free for anyone to build, sell or improve, and nobody can patent it out from under the game later.

    The deeper engineering questions and alternate designs considered are answered in the supplemental technical FAQ.

    Every field trial number on this page can be reproduced. The scorecard counts, the scrapers that produced them and the per match reports are all in the field trial repository on GitHub.

    Hit wicket is 0.4% of the total, a real but very thin slice. The bar and table views show it clearly.

    This is a lower bound, deliberately

    These counts come only from completed dismissals on the scorecard. They do not capture the many other times the bails are dislodged:

    • Run out and stumping attempts where the batter was not out
    • A bowled or hit wicket off a no ball
    • Any other ball that hits the stumps without a wicket being given

    The real number is higher. We count what can be verified from official records, and we make no claim that the device prevented any particular injury, only how often it would be engaged.
